# Biodiversity Solutions and Tradeoffs

## Hook

A town wants to protect a wetland and also needs a walking path. One plan is cheap but cuts through nesting habitat. Another costs more but curves around the sensitive area.

Science does not just ask, "Which plan sounds nice?" It asks, "Which plan best meets the goal with evidence and acceptable tradeoffs?"

## Big Idea

Biodiversity solutions should be judged with evidence. Good designs reduce harm, protect ecosystem functions, and consider constraints like cost, safety, time, and community needs.

Memory anchor: **Best solution means evidence plus tradeoffs.**

## Learn It

Biodiversity means the variety of living things in an ecosystem. Human actions can reduce biodiversity, but people can also design solutions.

A strong solution plan includes:

- a clear problem
- data about the ecosystem
- a design goal
- constraints and tradeoffs
- evidence showing whether the plan worked

## Build The Model

Use this design chain:

`impact data -> design goal -> possible solutions -> tradeoff comparison -> evidence-based choice`

Example:

`pollinator decline -> protect food-web support -> native plant corridor -> compare cost and habitat benefit -> monitor pollinator counts`

## Memory Anchors

- **Biodiversity supports stability.**
- **Solutions need evidence.**
- **Constraints are real.**
- **Tradeoffs do not mean failure.**

## Evidence Rules

A strong solution claim should include:

- before-and-after data
- which organisms or ecosystem functions are affected
- which constraint matters most
- how the design reduces harm

## Common Traps

- Trap: "The best solution is always the cheapest."  
  Fix: Cost matters, but evidence of impact and effectiveness matters too.
- Trap: "A solution must be perfect to be useful."  
  Fix: Real designs often improve outcomes while managing tradeoffs.
- Trap: "A nice-sounding plan is enough."  
  Fix: Science and engineering require data.

## Pause And Think

Answer these without looking back:

- What data would show a biodiversity problem?
- Why do constraints matter in design?
- How can a solution be useful even if it has tradeoffs?

## Apply It

A school notices fewer native bees near campus. Students propose replacing part of a lawn with native flowers. A strong test compares bee counts before and after, while also considering cost, water use, and maintenance.

## 3-2-1 Memory Check

3 things to remember:

- Biodiversity solutions should be evidence-based.
- Design choices have constraints and tradeoffs.
- Monitoring data helps show whether a solution works.

2 traps to avoid:

- Do not choose only by what sounds good.
- Do not reject every solution because it is not perfect.

1 explain-it prompt:

- How would you decide which wetland protection plan is strongest?
