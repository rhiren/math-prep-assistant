# Angle-Angle Similarity

## Big Idea

Two triangles are similar when they have the same shape. For triangles, you do not need to check every side and every angle.

If two angles in one triangle match two angles in another triangle, the triangles are similar. This is called Angle-Angle similarity, or AA similarity.

Why does that work? Every triangle has angles that add to `180` degrees. If two angle pairs already match, the third angle pair must match too.

## What AA Proves

AA similarity proves that two triangles have the same shape.

It does not prove that the triangles are the same size.

After AA proves similarity:

- corresponding angles are congruent
- corresponding side lengths are proportional
- one triangle may be an enlargement or reduction of the other
- a scale factor can connect matching sides

## Worked Example

Triangle A has angles `40` degrees and `65` degrees.

Triangle B has angles `40` degrees and `65` degrees.

Two angle pairs match, so the triangles are similar by AA.

The third angle in each triangle is:

`180 - 40 - 65 = 75`

That means all three angle pairs match, even though AA only needed two.

## Not Enough Evidence

One matching angle pair is not enough.

For example, many triangles can have a `50` degree angle. One could be tall and narrow, while another could be much wider. Sharing one angle does not force the whole shape to match.

AA needs two matching angle pairs.

## Using Parallel Lines

AA often appears in diagrams with parallel lines.

If a smaller triangle is formed inside a larger triangle and one segment is parallel to a side of the larger triangle, corresponding angles match. The triangles also often share one angle.

That gives two matching angle pairs, so AA proves the triangles are similar.

## Angle Correspondence

When triangles are similar, the order of the letters tells you which angles and sides match.

If `Triangle ABC ~ Triangle DEF`, then:

- angle `A` matches angle `D`
- angle `B` matches angle `E`
- angle `C` matches angle `F`
- side `AB` matches side `DE`
- side `BC` matches side `EF`
- side `AC` matches side `DF`

The order matters. A wrong correspondence can lead to a wrong proportion later.

## Common Traps

- Do not say triangles are congruent from AA. AA proves similarity, not same size.
- Do not use only one matching angle pair.
- Do not use the triangle angle sum alone as proof. Every triangle sums to `180` degrees.
- Do not mix up corresponding vertices after proving similarity.
- Do not assume a diagram is drawn to scale unless the angle relationships are marked or can be reasoned from parallel lines.

## Strategy

| Step | Question to ask |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Which two angle pairs match? |
| 2 | Are the angle matches given, calculated, vertical, alternate interior, or corresponding? |
| 3 | Can I name the similarity statement in the correct vertex order? |
| 4 | Am I proving similarity, not congruence? |
| 5 | If side lengths come next, which sides correspond? |

## Apply It

A support beam creates a small triangle inside a larger triangular frame. The beam is parallel to the base of the frame.

One angle is shared at the top of the frame. Another angle pair matches because the beam is parallel to the base.

Two angle pairs match, so the small triangle is similar to the large triangle by AA.

Once you know the triangles are similar, you can use proportional sides to find missing lengths.

## 3-2-1 Memory Check

3 things AA gives:

- same shape
- matching angles
- proportional corresponding sides

2 angle pairs:

- enough to prove triangle similarity
- enough because the third pair must match

1 warning:

AA does not prove congruence.
